01 · The brief

Who leaves, when,
and who should act?

The Business School asked for one view of five years of bachelor enrolments: is retention recovering, and where should support go? A dashboard answers the first half. The second half needs the people who would act, the moment they could act, and how many students each action would reach.

Who asks what

ExecutivesIs retention recovering, and where should support money go?
Course directorsWhich courses lose students, and where do transfers go?
Unit convenorsWhich units lead to students leaving, and why do students fail them?
Student advisersWho should we contact, and when?

What the first version missed

  • AveragesIt reported 89.6% retention and named a segment that holds 13% of leavers. Part-timers were 35% of 2024's students and 74% of its leavers.
  • TimingIt counted leavers a year after they went. Two in three showed a warning sign by the Session 2 census, and nearly half never enrolled in Session 2.
  • SizingIts recommendations named programs (mentoring, tutoring, alerts) without a sized group of students, and its what-if effects were assumptions. Every action here starts from a group of students the data can name.

02 · How I built it

From a dashboard
to a list of students to call.

The Power BI dashboard answers where retention stands. The re-analysis rebuilds the data one student-year at a time, session by session, so every finding comes with a trigger, a date and a headcount.

01

Model

A star schema in Snowflake.

  • Enrolment and retention fact tables
  • Student, course, unit, year, faculty and WAM dimensions
  • One grain per fact, documented
02

Measure

The University's own formula.

  • Retained ÷ (enrolled − completers), the "new normal" method
  • Course, faculty and university levels
  • Checked against the data dictionary
03

Dashboard

Five pages, three audiences.

  • Executives, course directors, unit convenors
  • Drill-through from course to unit
  • A what-if page for support programs
04

Go deeper

Student-year, session by session.

  • 34,756 student-years, completers excluded
  • Session pattern, load change and fail type per student
  • Every rate shown with its headcount
05

Predict

A point-in-time model.

  • Only facts known by the Session 2 census
  • Trained on 2020–2022, tested on 2024
  • Judged by leavers caught at a fixed capacity

03 · What the data says

Six findings,
each with a headcount.

Retention

Retained in any course the next year ÷ (enrolled − completers), the University's own method. Leavers are "not retained in year X + 1".

Unit of analysis

One row per student per year: 34,756 student-years from 2020 to 2024, completers excluded. 2025 has no outcome yet.

Read with care

Signals mark who is likely to leave, not why. Each action pairs a signal with a test of whether contact changes the outcome.
01

The recovery is real, but part-timers lag.

University retention fell from 90.5% in 2020 to 85.0% in 2022, then recovered to 89.6% in 2024. About 40% of the 2022 fall came from mix: continuing domestic part-timers, a group where three in ten leave, grew from 15.5% to 20.5% of students. The rest came from higher leaving rates, most sharply among international students, whose rate doubled from 7.4% to 14.7%.

Every year, part-timers retain far below full-timers: 78.1% against 95.8% in 2024. Over five years, part-timers were 36% of students and 73% of leavers.

So whatThe faculty-wide number is back to its pre-dip level, but the gap between study loads hasn't closed. Support aimed at "all students" spends most of its effort where few students leave.
02

Leavers are mostly part-timers, and most are on campus.

In 2024, continuing part-timers were 18% of students and 46% of leavers; a quarter of them left. New part-timers added another 28% of leavers. Full-timers in their second year or later were 38% of students and 9% of leavers.

The first version flagged single-degree, part-time, external students. That segment holds 13% of leavers: 61% of part-time leavers studied on campus.

So whatPart-time study, not online study, is the risk to manage. Timetables, Session 2 enrolment and completion planning for on-campus part-timers will reach more leavers than any online-only program.
03

Most leaving shows by the Session 2 census.

Four signals are visible by mid-year. Part-timers who take Session 1 but don't enrol in Session 2 leave at 51%. A fail-absent grade in Session 1 means 38%; a new student failing or withdrawing from a first-year core unit, 29%; a drop from full- to part-time, 22.5%. The average is 11.9%.

In 2024, the four signals flagged 1,455 students (22%) who included 437 of the 685 leavers (64%). The other 5,152 students lost 4.8%.

So whatThe University already holds the list. A weekly report of these four signals tells advisers who to call months before the official rate records the loss.
04

In the big first-year units, most fails are no-shows.

Six core units carry first year: ECON1020, ACCG1000, ACST1001, MGMT1002, STAT1250 and MKTG1001. In 2024, 783 of their 1,153 fails (68%) were fail-absent: students who stopped submitting or sitting assessments, not students who tried and failed.

The damage compounds. New students who failed or withdrew from one of the six left at 17.3%, two at 26.7%, three or more at 38.4%, against 7.2% for those who passed every one they took.

So whatExtra tutoring reaches students who turn up. The bigger group stops turning up, so the first fix is following up the first missed assessment in weeks 3–6.
05

Course alarms were small-sample noise.

Across the fifteen courses with at least 500 student-years, the share leaving each year runs from 5.4% to 15.2%. The Bachelor of Economics loses 13.0%, not the 43.8% the first version reported. The dashboard's 100% and 50% drop-out rates came from courses with one and four students in 2024; 47 of 83 courses had fewer than 20 student-years in five years.

Size matters more than rate. The Bachelor of Commerce holds 45% of all leavers. The Bachelor of Business has the highest rate among large courses (15.2%) with no more part-timers than Commerce (38.6% against 39.5%).

So whatPilot in Commerce, where a change moves the faculty number, and look inside Business, where the higher rate isn't explained by study load.
06

A model only helps at a set capacity.

Nine in ten students stay, so predicting "stays" for everyone is already 89.6% accurate. The useful question is how many leavers sit in the group advisers can actually contact.

I rebuilt the model with only what is known by the Session 2 census, trained it on 2020–2022 and tested it on 2024. Flagging the top 22% of students catches 71% of leavers, against 64% for the four simple signals; the top 10% catches half. Session 2 load, Session 1 fails and being a new student carry the most weight.

So whatStart with the four signals: they're transparent and already capture most of the gain. Use the model to rank students within the list once it keeps beating the rule on a new year.

04 · The next 12 months

One signal, one owner,
one date.

Each action names the trigger, the students in scope and the measure. Sizes are 2024 headcounts; "kept" figures are arithmetic, not promises: what a 10-point cut in the group's leaving rate would mean.

Three signals, three calls, all before the Session 2 census. Together they flagged 419 of 2024's 685 leavers (61%).

01

Call every part-timer who skips Session 2

Do

In August, call part-timers who took Session 1 but have no Session 2 enrolment. Offer a lighter load, leave of absence or a Session 3 unit instead of a silent exit.

Why

601 students in 2024; 315 of them (52%) didn't return, 46% of all leavers. A 10-point cut keeps about 60 a year.

TrackShare re-enrolled by next Session 1
02

Contact fail-absent students within a week

Do

When Session 1 results post, contact every student with a fail-absent grade within five working days and agree a plan before the Session 2 census.

Why

724 students in 2024, 35% of whom left. Over five years, two fail-absent grades in a year meant 43% left.

TrackFail-absent students enrolled for Session 2
03

Make a load drop a conversation

Do

When a full-time student re-enrols part-time, book a completion-planning call before census.

Why

Dropping to part-time raised the leaving rate from 3.8% to 22.5% (2020–2024). 512 students did it in 2024.

TrackCompletion plans agreed per load drop

Most fails in the six first-year core units are no-shows. Find them early, tutor the ones who try, and give new students a second run.

04

Chase the first missed assessment

Do

In weeks 3–6, flag students who miss the first assessment in the six units, and have their tutor follow up personally.

Why

In 2024, 783 of 1,153 fails in these units (68%) were fail-absent: students who stopped turning up.

TrackFail-absent rate per unit, each session
05

Point tutoring at attempted fails

Do

Run peer-assisted study where students sit the work and still fail: MGMT1002 (136 such fails in 2024), ACST1001 (86) and STAT1250 (72).

Why

Tutoring helps students who are there. It can't reach the no-shows, so it belongs where attempted fails cluster.

TrackF-grade rate in those three units
06

Give new students a second run

Do

Re-enrol new students who fail or withdraw from one of the six in Session 1 into a supported repeat, with an adviser check-in.

Why

One such unit meant 17.3% left and three or more meant 38.4%, against 7.2% for new students who passed every one they took. 313 students fitted this signal in 2024.

TrackRetention of supported-repeat students

Course-level effort should follow size and evidence, not alarming percentages from tiny courses. Pilot where it counts and investigate the outlier.

07

Pilot in the Bachelor of Commerce

Do

Run actions 01–03 in Commerce first, with the other large courses as the comparison.

Why

Commerce holds 45% of all leavers (1,931 over 2020–2024), so a result there moves the faculty number.

TrackCommerce retention against comparison courses
08

Look inside the Bachelor of Business

Do

Review the first-year sequence, timetable and part-time pathway of the Bachelor of Business.

Why

It loses 15.2% a year, the most of any large course, with a part-time share (38.6%) no higher than Commerce's, so the cause sits inside the course.

TrackBusiness retention, new and continuing
09

Plan combined-degree switches

Do

At the end of first year, offer combined-degree students a credit-mapped move into one of their two degrees.

Why

45% of combined-degree students who move to another course already pick one of its two halves; a planned switch beats a semester of drift.

TrackPlanned switches against exits

Fund the work by the number of students in scope, measure it fairly, and judge models by who they put on the list. Three decisions for executives and the BI team.

10

Report retention by session

Do

Add a monthly measure to the dashboard: Session 2 enrolment among Session 1 part-timers.

Why

Only 57.5% of 2024's Session 1 part-timers enrolled for Session 2. The loss shows in August; the official rate reports it a year later.

TrackSession 2 enrolment of Session 1 part-timers
11

Fund outreach by students in scope

Do

Size adviser capacity to about 1,450 flagged students a year, and contact them in random order over two weeks to measure the effect.

Why

The four signals flagged 22% of 2024's students and 64% of its leavers. Random contact order is a fair test that withholds help from no one.

TrackLeavers per 100 flagged, early against late contact
12

Judge models by leavers caught

Do

Report any risk model as the share of leavers in the top 15–20% it flags, tested on a later year.

Why

Predicting "stays" for everyone is 89.6% accurate, so accuracy can't show whether a model helps.

TrackLeavers caught at capacity, each year

05 · Three-year strategy

Find them early, keep them enrolled,
fix the gateways.

The 12-month actions test what works. The strategy builds it into how the School runs: an early list that improves each year, a part-time experience designed on purpose and first-year units that catch no-shows in week three. Every 5-point cut in the flagged group's leaving rate keeps about 73 students a year, about one point of university retention.

What I'd add next

  • Learning-platform and submission data, the signal that arrives before a fail-absent grade.
  • Reasons for leaving from an exit survey, so advice matches why students go.
  • Cost per contact, to compare outreach and tutoring by students kept per dollar.
  • Leave and Session 3 outcomes, to separate students pausing from students leaving.

How I'd run the next retention analysis

Ask who, when and how many

A rate says how big a problem is; a headcount and a date say what to do about it.

Count leavers, not only rates

The external segment had the worst rate. On-campus part-timers had the most leavers.

Distrust small denominators

A 100% drop-out rate from a course with a handful of students is an anecdote, not a finding.

Score a model at a capacity

Advisers can contact a few hundred students, so judge a model by who it puts on that list.
